# "The Grill That Grounded a Stryker": original script v1 (2026-11-06)

Format: a vertical cartoon 3D short (1080x1920, 30 fps, about 60 s, about 3.7 words/s), made with the
cartoon-3d-short skill (flat 2D cartoon characters in a painted 3D world, three.js inside Remotion).
The story, characters and voice are our own. There are no captions or labels in the picture.

## Facts and sources
- **The 96-hour goal.** In October 1999 Army Chief of Staff Gen. Eric Shinseki set out to deploy a brigade anywhere
  in the world in 96 hours (4 days). The interim armored vehicle for those brigades was to be carried by a C-130
  Hercules. The weight limit was 38,000 lb, or 19 short tons.
  Sources: Wikipedia "Stryker"; GAO-04-925.
- **The vehicle.** The M1126 Stryker infantry carrier is 8x8 wheeled. It carries a 9-soldier squad plus a 2-person
  crew. Its top speed is about 60 mph (97 km/h). Main battle tanks such as the M1 Abrams (~60-70 t) are far too heavy
  for a C-130.
- **The name.** The Army announced it in February 2002. It honors two unrelated Medal of Honor recipients, both
  awarded posthumously: PFC Stuart S. Stryker (WWII, near Wesel, Germany, 1945) and Spc. 4 Robert F. Stryker
  (Vietnam, near Loc Ninh, 1967).
  Sources: Army release via DefenseLink; GlobalSecurity; Wikipedia.
- **The tight fit.** The vehicle height is about 8 ft 8 in. The C-130 cargo opening is about 9 ft. That leaves inches
  to spare.
- **Iraq and the cage.** Strykers deployed to Iraq in late 2003. Slat ("bar") armor was added against RPGs. It looks
  like a grill. It adds about 5,000 lb (~2.5 short tons), more weight than a C-130 can carry it with. With the slat
  cage on, the vehicle also no longer fits the C-130 fuselage, so caged Strykers fly in bigger aircraft such as the C-17.
  Sources: Wikipedia "Stryker"; GAO-04-925; Honolulu Star-Bulletin, 2004-08-15 "Stryker's bulk limits".
- **How slats work.** The RPG-7's PG-7V warhead has a piezoelectric nose fuze. The firing current runs from the nose,
  through the warhead's outer and inner cones, to the base detonator. Slats catch the warhead and pinch the cone,
  which shorts or breaks that circuit, so it often fails to go off. Commonly cited effectiveness is about 50-60 %.

## Voice-over
The Army built a combat vehicle to fit inside a cargo plane, then gave it a grill that made it too big to fly.
It's 1999, and the Army's top general wants a whole brigade anywhere on Earth in four days.
Tanks are way too heavy for that, so engineers build something lighter: eight big wheels, room for nine soldiers,
and sixty miles an hour.
They name it the Stryker, after two soldiers who both earned the Medal of Honor, and weren't even related.
It squeezes into a C-130 with only inches to spare.
Then it rolls into Iraq, and meets rocket-propelled grenades.
So the crews wrap it in a steel cage that looks exactly like a giant barbecue grill.
Wait, a grill? Against rockets?
You see, that warhead only fires when a tiny electric signal runs from its nose to its back.
Ever stepped on a garden hose? The water stops before it reaches the end.
The bars pinch the warhead's nose the same way, so the signal never makes it.
About half the time, the rocket just bounces off as a dud.
There's just one problem. The cage adds two and a half tons, and makes the Stryker wider.
So the vehicle built around one airplane now needs a bigger one, just to carry its barbecue grill.
